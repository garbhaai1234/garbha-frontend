You are entitled to understand how your embryos will be assessed and chosen, and a good IVF clinic will be glad you asked. Embryo selection involves several decisions — how embryos are graded, who makes the call, how many to transfer, whether to freeze, and whether to pay for extra tests or technologies — and the evidence behind each is uneven. Knowing the right questions helps you have an informed conversation with your clinician rather than simply receiving a verdict.

Below are eight questions worth asking, why each matters, and what a thoughtful answer tends to sound like. This guide does not tell you which treatment to choose; that depends on your history and belongs in a conversation with your fertility specialist.

> - ESHRE's 2023 good practice recommendations on IVF add-ons found that **none of its 42 recommendations could be based on high-quality evidence**, and only four on moderate-quality evidence.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> It is reasonable to ask what the evidence is for anything offered at extra cost.
> - A Cochrane review found that transferring one embryo at a time **probably reduces multiple pregnancy**, and that when a single transfer is followed by a further single transfer, the cumulative live birth rate may be little or no different from one double transfer, at least in women with a good prognosis.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>
> - In a randomised trial of 1,575 participants, time-lapse imaging for embryo culture and selection **did not significantly increase the odds of live birth** compared with standard care.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>
> - In a randomised trial of 1,212 women with three or more good-quality blastocysts, conventional IVF gave a cumulative live birth rate that was **noninferior to PGT-A** (81.8% versus 77.2%).<sup>[4](#ref4)</sup>

## Why should I ask about embryo selection at all?

Because it is one of the few steps in IVF that is largely invisible to you. You attend scans and procedures, but the assessment of your embryos happens in the laboratory, often over several days, and you usually hear only the result: a grade, a recommendation, a number frozen.

Asking helps you understand what your embryos' grades do and do not mean, and gives you a fair basis for weighing any extra tests or technologies you are offered. ESHRE's add-on recommendations were written, in the working group's own words, to enable patients "to make informed decisions based on realistic expectations".<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

## 1. How do you grade embryos, and what does my embryo's grade mean?

**Why it matters.** Most laboratories grade Day-5 blastocysts using a system that describes how far the embryo has expanded and the appearance of two groups of cells: the inner cell mass, which becomes the fetus, and the trophectoderm, which becomes the placenta. The ESHRE/ALPHA Istanbul consensus, updated in 2025, sets out 20 recommendations on morphological criteria and on the frequency and timing of assessments, to help ensure consistency.<sup>[6](#ref6)</sup> It also notes that several criteria have not been well studied, so some are recommended only for ranking embryos rather than grading them, or not at all.<sup>[6](#ref6)</sup>

**What a good answer sounds like.** A clear explanation of the grading system the laboratory uses, at what times embryos are assessed, and what your specific grades describe. A good answer will also be honest that a grade shifts the odds rather than determining the outcome, and that a lower-graded embryo is not a failed embryo.

## 2. Who reviews the embryos, and does more than one embryologist look at the decision?

**Why it matters.** Grading is an expert visual judgement, and skilled judgements vary. We have written separately about [how consistent embryo grading is](/blog/how-consistent-is-embryo-grading) — in short, experienced embryologists do not always give the same embryo the same grade, and the disagreement matters most when several embryos look similar.

**What a good answer sounds like.** A description of who is involved: the seniority of the embryologists, whether a second embryologist checks the selection in borderline cases, and how the laboratory monitors grading consistency as part of its quality management. A laboratory that measures and discusses its own variability is showing you something reassuring, not something worrying.

## 3. Do you use time-lapse imaging, and what does it add?

**Why it matters.** Time-lapse incubators photograph embryos at regular intervals without removing them from the incubator, so embryologists can review how each embryo developed rather than seeing it at one or two fixed moments. It is an appealing technology, and it can be useful for the laboratory's assessment. But the question for you is whether it changes the chance of a baby.

The best evidence so far is the TILT trial, a multicentre, double-blind randomised trial at seven IVF centres in the UK and Hong Kong. It randomised 1,575 participants to time-lapse imaging for culture and selection, time-lapse for undisturbed culture only, or standard care. Live birth rates were 33.7%, 36.6% and 33.0% respectively, and the authors concluded that time-lapse imaging for embryo culture and selection **does not significantly increase the odds of live birth** compared with standard care.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>

**What a good answer sounds like.** An honest description of what time-lapse does in that particular laboratory, whether it is part of the standard service or an extra cost, and an acknowledgement that it has not been shown to increase live birth rates.

## 4. Which add-ons are you recommending for me, and what is the evidence?

**Why it matters.** "Add-ons" are optional extras offered on top of standard IVF — tests, drugs, laboratory techniques and procedures — usually at additional cost, and usually with the stated aim of improving pregnancy or live birth rates.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> ESHRE convened a multidisciplinary working group, including patient representatives, to review the evidence. It produced 42 recommendations; **none could be based on high-quality evidence, and only four on moderate-quality evidence**, meaning that 95% rested on low-quality trials, observational data, professional experience or consensus.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

That does not mean every add-on is useless. It means the evidence is often thin, and it is sensible to ask.

**What a good answer sounds like.** A specific reason why an add-on is being suggested for *you*, rather than for everyone; a plain statement of what the evidence shows and how strong it is; the cost; and any risks. A clinic that is comfortable saying "the evidence for this is limited" is being straight with you.

## 5. How many embryos do you recommend transferring, and why?

**Why it matters.** Transferring more than one embryo raises the chance of twins or more, and multiple pregnancy carries higher risks for mother and babies. A Cochrane review of 17 randomised trials involving 2,505 women examined this trade-off.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup> Comparing one cycle of single embryo transfer with one cycle of double embryo transfer, live birth per fresh cycle may be lower with a single transfer. But when a single transfer was followed by a further single transfer — fresh or frozen — the cumulative live birth rate may be little or no different from one double transfer, while the multiple pregnancy rate was probably reduced.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup> The reviewers rated the evidence as low to moderate quality, and their conclusion applies at least to women with a good prognosis.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>

**What a good answer sounds like.** A recommendation tailored to your age, history and embryo quality, with a clear explanation of the chance of multiple pregnancy under each option and what the clinic's approach is to transferring remaining embryos in later cycles.

## 6. Should my embryos be transferred fresh, or should all of them be frozen?

**Why it matters.** Some clinics freeze all embryos and transfer one in a later cycle (a "freeze-all" approach) rather than transferring in the same cycle as egg collection. There are sound medical reasons to do this in some situations, such as reducing the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. As a routine approach for everyone, however, the evidence is more mixed.

In a multicentre randomised trial of 460 women with regular menstrual cycles in Denmark, Sweden and Spain, a freeze-all strategy **did not result in higher ongoing pregnancy or live birth rates** than fresh transfer (live birth 27.4% versus 28.7%), and time to pregnancy was longer in the freeze-all group.<sup>[7](#ref7)</sup> The authors advised caution about applying freeze-all indiscriminately when there is no apparent risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome.<sup>[7](#ref7)</sup>

**What a good answer sounds like.** A clear reason for the recommendation in your case — for example, your response to stimulation — rather than a blanket policy, along with what freezing means for your timeline.

## 7. Is PGT-A right for me?

**Why it matters.** Preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) involves taking a small biopsy from the embryo to check the number of chromosomes, with the aim of choosing a chromosomally normal embryo for transfer. It is widely offered and often costly, so it is worth understanding what it has and has not been shown to do.

In a multicentre randomised trial of 1,212 women aged 20 to 37 who each had three or more good-quality blastocysts, cumulative live births after up to three single transfers occurred in **77.2% of the PGT-A group and 81.8% of the conventional IVF group**. The authors concluded that conventional IVF was noninferior to PGT-A in this population.<sup>[4](#ref4)</sup> Clinical pregnancy loss was lower in the PGT-A group (8.7% versus 12.6%).<sup>[4](#ref4)</sup>

These results apply to the women studied — younger women with several good-quality blastocysts — and may not apply to every situation.

**What a good answer sounds like.** A discussion of why PGT-A is or is not being suggested for your particular circumstances, what it can and cannot tell you, what happens to embryos with uncertain results, and what it costs.

## 8. Do you use AI or software to help select embryos, and how is it used?

**Why it matters.** AI tools for embryo assessment are increasingly common, and it is reasonable to know whether one is involved in your care. The strongest trial so far randomised 1,066 patients across 14 IVF clinics in Australia and Europe to embryo selection by a deep-learning algorithm or by standard morphology. Clinical pregnancy was 46.5% with the algorithm and 48.2% with standard morphology, and the trial **could not demonstrate noninferiority** of the deep-learning approach.<sup>[5](#ref5)</sup>

In plain terms, the current evidence does not show that AI embryo selection leads to more pregnancies. Where AI tools can help is with consistency: giving the laboratory a reproducible second assessment in cases where human grading tends to vary.

**What a good answer sounds like.** A clear account of what the software does, whether its output is reviewed by an embryologist, who makes the final decision, and an honest statement that it supports rather than replaces professional judgement. Be cautious of any claim that a tool guarantees or dramatically increases success.

## How should I use these questions?

Pick the ones that matter to you and bring them to a consultation. Ask what the evidence shows, not just whether something "can help"; ask about cost and risk alongside benefit; and write the answers down. A good clinic will answer plainly and be comfortable saying "we don't know" where the evidence is uncertain.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it rude to ask my IVF clinic how they choose embryos?

No. Understanding your treatment is part of informed consent, and experienced clinicians expect these questions.

### Do add-ons improve my chance of having a baby?

For most add-ons, the evidence is limited. ESHRE's 2023 good practice recommendations reviewed add-ons in reproductive medicine and found that none of its 42 recommendations could be based on high-quality evidence.

### Is single embryo transfer less successful than double embryo transfer?

Per fresh cycle, a single transfer may give a lower live birth rate. But a Cochrane review found that when a single transfer is followed by a further single transfer, the cumulative live birth rate may be little or no different from one double transfer, while multiple pregnancies are probably reduced — at least in women with a good prognosis.

### Does time-lapse imaging increase live birth rates?

The largest randomised trial to date, TILT, found that time-lapse imaging for embryo culture and selection did not significantly increase the odds of live birth compared with standard care.

### Should everyone have PGT-A?

Not necessarily. In a large randomised trial of women aged 20 to 37 with three or more good-quality blastocysts, conventional IVF gave a cumulative live birth rate that was noninferior to PGT-A. Whether PGT-A is appropriate depends on individual circumstances, which is a discussion to have with your specialist.

### Does AI choose better embryos than an embryologist?

It has not been shown to. The largest randomised trial of AI embryo selection could not demonstrate noninferiority to standard morphology for clinical pregnancy. AI tools are best understood as consistent decision support for embryologists, who remain responsible for the final choice.
