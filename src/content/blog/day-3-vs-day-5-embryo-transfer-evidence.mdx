Transferring an embryo on Day 5 rather than Day 3 probably raises the chance of a live birth from that fresh transfer. Whether it raises a woman's overall chance of taking a baby home from one egg collection is a different question, and the evidence there is much less certain. The 2026 Cochrane review of 36 randomised trials rates the fresh-transfer benefit as **moderate certainty**, and the cumulative benefit as only **low certainty**.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> The largest trial to measure cumulative live birth directly found no difference at all.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>

Blastocyst transfer has become the default in many laboratories, and for good reasons. But "better per transfer" and "better per patient" are not the same claim, and the difference matters to embryologists planning a culture strategy and to couples deciding what to ask their clinic.

> ### Key takeaways
>
> - Live birth per **fresh** transfer is probably higher with blastocyst transfer: if 32% of women have a live birth after a fresh Day-3 transfer, **37% to 43%** would after a fresh blastocyst transfer (moderate certainty).<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>
> - **Cumulative** live birth within 12 months, counting fresh and frozen transfers from one egg collection, *may* be higher with blastocyst transfer (OR 1.24), but the evidence is of **low certainty**.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>
> - In the largest trial, 1,202 women with at least four embryos had near-identical cumulative live birth rates: **58.9% (Day 5) versus 58.4% (Day 3)**.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>
> - With extended culture, having **no embryo to transfer** may be more likely (OR 2.69) and having spare embryos to freeze may be less likely (OR 0.44), both low certainty.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>
> - Cumulative **preterm birth** is probably higher after blastocyst transfer (moderate certainty).<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> Almost none of this evidence applies to women with a poor prognosis, who were underrepresented in the trials.

## What is the difference between a Day-3 and a Day-5 embryo?

A Day-3 embryo, also called a cleavage-stage embryo, is typically a small cluster of around six to eight cells that still sit inside the outer shell, the zona pellucida. It has only recently begun to rely on its own genes to direct development.

By Day 5 or Day 6, an embryo that keeps developing becomes a blastocyst: a fluid-filled structure of many more cells, already separated into an inner cell mass, which becomes the fetus, and the trophectoderm, which becomes the placenta. Our guide to [embryo development from Day 1 to the Day-5 blastocyst](/blog/ai-embryo-grading-explained-day-1-to-blastocyst) walks through each stage.

Not every embryo that looks healthy on Day 3 will reach the blastocyst stage in the laboratory. That single fact explains most of the debate that follows.

## Why would waiting until Day 5 help?

The Cochrane authors describe two proposed mechanisms.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> The first is **synchrony**: in a natural conception, the embryo reaches the uterus at around the blastocyst stage, so a Day-5 transfer may match the embryo to the endometrium more closely. The second is **self-selection**: embryos that cannot sustain development drop out during extended culture, leaving the embryologist a smaller, better-characterised group to choose from.

The review is candid that it is still uncertain whether extended culture gives a genuine biological advantage, or mainly lets the laboratory see which embryos were going to fail anyway.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> That distinction is important. If the benefit is mostly selection, it should show up per transfer but largely disappear once every embryo from the cycle has been used.

## What does the latest Cochrane review find?

The January 2026 update of the Cochrane review included **36 parallel-group randomised trials with 8,389 participants**, with searches run to October 2024.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> Blastocyst transfer was defined as Day 5 to 6 and cleavage-stage transfer as Day 2 to 3. The main findings, with their GRADE certainty ratings:

- **Live birth per fresh transfer:** probably higher with blastocyst transfer (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.56; 19 studies, 4,787 women; *moderate certainty*). A 32% live birth rate after fresh cleavage-stage transfer would correspond to 37% to 43% after fresh blastocyst transfer.
- **Clinical pregnancy per fresh transfer:** may be higher (OR 1.31; 36 studies, 8,389 women; *low certainty*).
- **Miscarriage per fresh transfer:** may show little or no difference (OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.33; *low certainty*).
- **Cumulative live birth within 12 months:** may be higher with blastocyst transfer (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.47; 3 studies, 2,328 women; *low certainty*). If 62% of women have a live birth after cleavage-stage transfer, 63% to 70% would with blastocyst transfer.
- **Cumulative preterm birth:** probably higher with blastocyst transfer (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.14 to 2.59; 2 studies, 2,194 women; *moderate certainty*). An illustrative 3.5% risk would become 3.9% to 8.5%.
- **Embryos available to freeze:** may be less often available after blastocyst culture (OR 0.44; *low certainty*).
- **Failure to transfer:** may be more common in the blastocyst group (OR 2.69, 95% CI 1.94 to 3.73; *low certainty*).

The review notes that certainty was low for most outcomes, limited by risk of bias, heterogeneity, imprecision and poor reporting of how randomisation was done.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

## Does Day 5 transfer increase the overall chance of a baby?

This is the question that matters most to patients, and it is where the evidence is weakest.

Only three trials in the Cochrane review reported cumulative live birth, and their results disagreed substantially (I² = 70%). The review attributes that heterogeneity to one large trial in which women went through an average of two or more transfers, and which found little or no difference between strategies.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

That trial, published in the *BMJ* in 2024, randomised 1,202 women across 21 Dutch hospitals and clinics.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup> All had at least four embryos on Day 2. Transfers were performed on Day 5 or Day 3, surplus embryos were frozen, and all frozen transfers within a year were counted. The cumulative live birth rate was **58.9% in the blastocyst group and 58.4% in the cleavage group** (risk ratio 1.01, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.22).

The trial did find real differences along the way. The blastocyst group had a higher live birth rate after the fresh transfer, a lower cumulative pregnancy loss rate, and needed fewer transfers on average to reach a live birth (**1.55 versus 1.82**).<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup> In other words, Day-5 transfer got women to the same destination with fewer transfers, but it did not get more of them there.

The Cochrane authors add an important caveat: within a 12-month window, some women will still have frozen embryos left. Cumulative figures could change once every embryo has been used.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

## What happens if no embryo survives to Day 5?

This is the practical risk of extended culture. The Cochrane review found that failure to transfer may be more common with blastocyst culture (OR 2.69, *low certainty*), and that women may be less likely to have spare embryos to freeze (OR 0.44, *low certainty*).<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

For a patient, reaching Day 5 with no embryo to transfer is a hard outcome, even if the embryos that stopped developing may never have led to a pregnancy. Whether those embryos would have implanted if transferred on Day 3 is exactly the unresolved biological question described above. The reviewers conclude that it remains unclear whether the differences in freezing and failure to transfer lead to higher cumulative live birth, or simply to longer treatment with more cost and a longer time to pregnancy.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

## Are there differences in pregnancy and baby outcomes?

The randomised evidence points to a modest increase in preterm birth. The Cochrane review rates the increase in cumulative preterm birth after blastocyst transfer as moderate certainty,<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> and the Dutch trial found more moderate preterm births (32 to under 37 weeks) among singletons in the blastocyst group (risk ratio 1.87, 95% CI 1.05 to 3.34).<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>

A larger body of observational data points the same way. A systematic review and cumulative meta-analysis of 35 studies covering 520,769 singleton pregnancies found that fresh blastocyst transfer was associated with a higher risk of babies being large for gestational age and of very preterm birth compared with fresh cleavage-stage transfer.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup> After frozen blastocyst transfer, risks of large-for-gestational-age babies, preterm birth and caesarean section were higher, while risks of small-for-gestational-age babies and perinatal mortality were lower.

The authors describe the evidence as predominantly reassuring but of low to very low quality, and suggest that cleavage-stage transfer may be worth considering for some women based on their individual risk profile.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup> These are associations in observational data, not proof that blastocyst culture causes these outcomes.

## Who does this evidence apply to?

Mainly to women with a good prognosis. The Cochrane review states that none of its findings should be generalised to women with a poor prognosis, who were underrepresented in most of the included trials.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> The Dutch trial only enrolled women with at least four embryos available on Day 2.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>

That leaves a real gap for the patients for whom the decision is hardest: older women, women with few eggs, and women whose embryos have stopped developing in earlier cycles. For them, the balance between better selection and a higher risk of no transfer has not been tested properly in trials.

Whatever the day of transfer, the number of embryos transferred remains a separate decision. ESHRE's 2024 guideline concludes that no clinical or embryological factor on its own justifies double embryo transfer rather than elective single embryo transfer.<sup>[4](#ref4)</sup>

## What do patients actually care about?

When 164 Dutch women starting IVF were asked to weigh up hypothetical treatments in a discrete choice experiment, the **cumulative live birth rate** was the most important factor in their choice. The number of embryos available for transfer also shaped their preferences, while the number of transfers needed to get pregnant did not, on average.<sup>[5](#ref5)</sup> Older women and women who already had children gave more weight to the impact of treatment on their quality of life.

That is a useful reminder for counselling. The outcome patients value most is the one on which the Day 3 versus Day 5 evidence is least certain.

## What this means for your laboratory

- **Be precise about the outcome you quote.** "Blastocyst transfer improves live birth" is supported per fresh transfer at moderate certainty. Per egg collection, the evidence is low certainty and the largest trial showed no difference.
- **Track your own failure-to-transfer and freezing rates** by culture strategy. They are the practical cost of extended culture, and they vary with laboratory conditions and patient mix.
- **Individualise for the harder cases.** The trials mostly enrolled good-prognosis patients. For women with few embryos, the evidence does not tell you which strategy is better, so the decision should be explicit and documented.
- **Make the Day-3 assessment count.** When a laboratory transfers or freezes at cleavage stage, it selects with less information than at Day 5. A consistent, reproducible assessment of cleavage-stage embryos, including their developmental timings, matters more in that setting. Our article on [how consistent embryo grading is](/blog/how-consistent-is-embryo-grading) sets out how much grading can vary between embryologists.
- **Discuss perinatal outcomes openly.** The small increase in preterm birth is part of the evidence and belongs in patient counselling.

## What this means if you are a patient

If your clinic recommends a Day-5 transfer, that is a well-established approach supported by good evidence that each fresh transfer is more likely to succeed. If your clinic recommends a Day-3 transfer, that is not a second-best option. For some patients, particularly those with only a few embryos, it may be the more sensible choice, and the research does not show that it lowers your overall chance of a baby.

Useful questions to ask your clinic:

- Why do you recommend Day 3 or Day 5 for me specifically?
- What happens if none of my embryos reach Day 5?
- If there are spare embryos, at what stage will they be frozen?
- How many embryos do you plan to transfer, and why?

No transfer strategy guarantees a pregnancy, and a good clinic will explain the trade-offs rather than present one option as simply better.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a Day-5 embryo transfer better than a Day-3 transfer?

Per fresh transfer, probably yes. The 2026 Cochrane review found that live birth per fresh transfer is probably higher with blastocyst transfer (moderate certainty). Across all fresh and frozen transfers from one egg collection, the benefit is uncertain. The largest trial found cumulative live birth rates of 58.9% and 58.4%.

### Why do some clinics still transfer on Day 3?

Extended culture carries a higher risk that no embryo is available to transfer, and fewer embryos may be available to freeze. For some patients, particularly those with few embryos, a Day-3 transfer can be a reasonable choice. The trials mostly included women with a good prognosis, so evidence for other groups is limited.

### What are the chances that no embryo survives to Day 5?

It depends heavily on the number and quality of embryos and on the laboratory. The Cochrane review found that failure to transfer may be more common with blastocyst culture (odds ratio 2.69, low certainty), but it does not give a single figure that applies to every patient. Your clinic can tell you what it sees in cases like yours.

### Does blastocyst transfer affect the baby's health?

The evidence suggests a modest increase in preterm birth after blastocyst transfer (moderate certainty in the Cochrane review). Observational studies also link blastocyst transfer to a higher chance of a baby being large for gestational age. Most of this evidence is reassuring overall, but it is a reasonable topic to discuss with your doctor.

### Should more than one embryo be transferred on Day 3 to make up the difference?

ESHRE's 2024 guideline concludes that no clinical or embryological factor on its own justifies transferring two embryos instead of one. Transferring more embryos raises the risk of multiple pregnancy and its complications, whatever the day of transfer.

### Can AI decide whether I should have a Day-3 or Day-5 transfer?

No. AI tools can provide a consistent assessment of each embryo at each stage to support the embryologist, but the timing of transfer is a clinical decision based on your history, your embryos and your preferences, made by your fertility team with you.
