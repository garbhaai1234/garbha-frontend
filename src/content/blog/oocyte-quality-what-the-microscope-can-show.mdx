Most of what makes an egg capable of becoming a baby cannot be seen down a standard laboratory microscope. A systematic review of 50 studies on oocyte morphology and IVF outcomes examined ten different features — from the zona pellucida to the polar body to the texture of the cytoplasm — and found that **none of them was unanimously shown to predict an oocyte's developmental competence**.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup> Embryologists look closely at every egg they handle. What they see is real, but it tells them less than most people assume.

This matters because oocyte quality is the foundation everything else in an IVF cycle is built on. An embryo cannot be better than the egg it came from. So it is worth being precise about what the microscope actually shows, what the evidence says those observations are worth, and what remains invisible.

> ### Key takeaways
>
> - A systematic review of **50 studies** found that no single oocyte morphological feature was unanimously shown to predict developmental competence.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>
> - A meta-analysis of **14 studies (3,688 ICSI cycles)** linked only four features — a large first polar body, a large perivitelline space, refractile bodies and vacuoles — to lower fertilisation, and found the effect on implantation and pregnancy unclear.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>
> - The 2025 ESHRE/ALPHA Istanbul consensus update concluded that oocytes with atypical zona, perivitelline space, polar body or cytoplasmic granularity are **suitable for clinical use**.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>
> - The strongest known influence on egg competence is not visible at all. In **15,169** embryo biopsies, the chance of a cohort containing no chromosomally normal embryo was 2–6% at ages 26–37, **33% at 42 and 53% at 44**.<sup>[5](#ref5)</sup>
> - AI oocyte assessment is early. One of the largest models to date, trained on 37,133 oocyte images, predicted blastocyst development with an AUC of **0.63 on external data** — better than chance, far from definitive.<sup>[7](#ref7)</sup>

## What does an embryologist actually look at in an egg?

After egg collection, the surrounding cumulus cells are usually removed before ICSI, and the embryologist examines each oocyte under a light microscope. The first question is maturity: a mature (metaphase II, or MII) egg has extruded its first polar body, a small cell visible in the space just inside the shell. Only mature eggs are normally injected.

Beyond maturity, the embryologist notes the appearance of structures both outside and inside the egg. The 2025 Istanbul consensus update groups them into two families:<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>

- **Extra-cytoplasmic features:** the cumulus–oocyte complex, the zona pellucida (the protective outer shell), the perivitelline space (the gap between the shell and the egg), the first polar body, and the egg's overall shape and size.
- **Intra-cytoplasmic features:** vacuoles, refractile bodies, aggregates of smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER-a), central granularity and the colour of the cytoplasm.

Any of these can look "atypical". The practical question is whether an atypical appearance actually means a less capable egg.

## Which oocyte features are linked to worse outcomes?

A few, weakly, and mostly for early outcomes rather than for pregnancy.

The clearest quantitative summary comes from a meta-analysis of 14 studies covering 3,688 ICSI cycles. It found that the probability of an oocyte being fertilised was significantly reduced by the presence of a **large first polar body** (odds ratio 0.29), a **large perivitelline space** (0.86), **refractile bodies** (0.66) or **vacuoles** (0.59). No other morphological abnormality it investigated showed a significant relationship with ICSI outcomes, and the authors noted that the effects of oocyte abnormalities on implantation and pregnancy rates remained unclear.<sup>[2](#ref2)</sup>

That distinction — fertilisation versus pregnancy — runs through the whole literature. An oocyte feature may make an egg slightly less likely to fertilise, but once it has fertilised and developed into a good embryo, the evidence that the original appearance still matters is thin.

## Which features turn out not to matter as much as they look?

Rather more than people expect. The 2025 update to the ESHRE/ALPHA Istanbul consensus was written by 17 experts on the basis of a systematic literature search, and it produced 20 recommendations on oocyte and embryo assessment.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup> Its conclusions on oocyte dysmorphisms are notably cautious:

- **Zona pellucida:** studies disagree; evidence was insufficient to support a negative prognosis, so oocytes with different zona phenotypes are considered suitable for clinical use.
- **Perivitelline space:** contradictory reports; oocytes with different perivitelline space phenotypes are considered suitable for clinical use.
- **Polar body:** no significant association between polar body fragmentation and fertilisation, and no reported association with implantation or clinical pregnancy; fragmented or large polar bodies are considered suitable for clinical use. A *disproportionately* large polar body, although very rare, deserves more attention.
- **Refractile bodies and cytoplasmic granularity:** evidence was insufficient to support a negative prognosis; these oocytes are considered suitable for clinical use.
- **Cytoplasmic colour:** the consensus described these observations as highly subjective and the data as too heterogeneous to draw any conclusion.

The consensus also states plainly that several oocyte and embryo criteria have not been well studied, which led either to a recommendation against using them for grading, or to using them only for ranking.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup> The working group's own practice survey found that only **35%** of respondents always applied the earlier 2011 recommendations to score oocytes.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>

Where the consensus does draw a line, it is modest. Giant oocytes should be excluded from clinical use, and embryos derived from mature oocytes free of large or multiple vacuoles, SER-a, disproportional shapes and very large first polar bodies should be *prioritised* — ranked higher, not the others discarded.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>

## What about smooth endoplasmic reticulum aggregates?

SER-a is the best illustration of how oocyte assessment has changed. The 2011 Istanbul consensus advised against injecting or inseminating these oocytes, because of reported adverse outcomes. A later panel moved to a case-by-case approach.<sup>[4](#ref4)</sup>

A 2019 review of 21 studies found that the literature does not unanimously support a negative association between SER-a and embryo development, implantation or treatment outcomes. It collated 112 reported neonatal outcomes after transfers involving at least one embryo from an SER-a-positive oocyte: 101 healthy babies, three live births with malformations, three neonatal deaths, one stillbirth and four medical terminations. The authors suggested fertilising all mature oocytes and giving priority to embryos from unaffected oocytes.<sup>[4](#ref4)</sup> The 2025 consensus now states that SER-a positive oocytes could be inseminated, based on a case-by-case evaluation.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>

Two further details are instructive. In a cohort of 1,662 cycles, SER-a affected **347 of 12,436 mature oocytes (2.8%)**, and its appearance was associated with longer ovarian stimulation — so it may say as much about the stimulation as about the woman's eggs.<sup>[6](#ref6)</sup> And the 2019 review cautioned that, given the poor resolution of light microscopy for detecting SER aggregates, oocytes that appear unaffected may not truly be free of them.<sup>[4](#ref4)</sup> Even a feature the microscope *can* show is not always shown reliably.

## What can't the microscope see?

The most important determinants of whether an egg can become a healthy embryo are not visible in a standard light-microscope image of the oocyte.

The clearest example is chromosomal normality — whether the egg, and the embryo it becomes, carries the correct number of chromosomes. That is not something a light-microscope image of an oocyte reveals, and its frequency tracks the woman's age rather than anything an embryologist can see. In a review of **15,169** consecutive trophectoderm biopsies, aneuploidy increased predictably after the age of 26. The rate of cohorts with *no* chromosomally normal embryo was lowest (2–6%) in women aged 26 to 37, rose to **33% at age 42** and to **53% at age 44**.<sup>[5](#ref5)</sup>

Eggs from a 43-year-old and a 30-year-old can look very similar under the microscope while carrying very different chances of leading to a chromosomally normal embryo. This is why age is central to any honest discussion of egg quality — and why no morphological assessment, human or automated, should be presented as a way around it.

Other structures, such as the meiotic spindle, are typically assessed with polarised-light microscopy rather than routine imaging, and even there the systematic evidence has not established consistent prognostic value.<sup>[1](#ref1)</sup>

## Can AI see more in an oocyte than an embryologist?

It may be able to extract more consistent information from the same image. Whether that information is clinically decisive is not yet established.

The most substantial published model in this area was trained on **37,133 static images** of mature oocytes from eight clinics in six countries, and asked to predict whether each egg would go on to form a blastocyst. On its internal test set it achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of **0.64**, with sensitivity of 0.65 and specificity of 0.55. On external data from two further clinics it held up at an **AUC of 0.63**. When its outputs were converted to a 0–10 score, higher-scoring oocytes were more likely to form blastocysts and good-quality blastocysts.<sup>[7](#ref7)</sup>

Those results deserve to be read both ways. An AUC around 0.63, confirmed on data from clinics the model had not seen, is a genuine signal — the image contains information about developmental potential. But an AUC of 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect, so this is a modest discriminator, predicting blastocyst formation rather than pregnancy or live birth. The authors themselves describe it as a first step.<sup>[7](#ref7)</sup>

Adoption is also early. In the practice survey that informed the 2025 Istanbul update, only **14%** of respondents reported using AI at all, mainly for embryo rather than oocyte assessment.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>

The honest position is the same one we took on [how consistent embryo grading is](/blog/how-consistent-is-embryo-grading): the strongest near-term case for software is consistency and documentation — measuring the same features the same way every time — rather than a claim to see an egg's future.

## What this means for your laboratory

- **Separate description from prognosis.** Record dysmorphisms carefully, but align how they influence decisions with the 2025 consensus: most atypical phenotypes are suitable for clinical use, and a small number justify lower priority rather than exclusion.
- **Standardise the vocabulary.** With only 35% of surveyed respondents consistently applying the earlier oocyte scoring recommendations, two labs — or two embryologists — may describe the same egg differently. Agree internal definitions and audit them.
- **Keep the oocyte record linked to the embryo.** Oocyte features are weak predictors on their own. Their value grows when they are traceable through fertilisation, cleavage and blastocyst outcome in your own data.
- **Look at stimulation as well as the egg.** Features such as SER-a appear to be associated with how the ovaries were stimulated, which makes them a feedback signal for the clinical team, not just a verdict on the patient.
- **Ask AI vendors what their model predicts, and where it was validated.** Blastocyst formation is not live birth, and internal accuracy is not external accuracy.

## What this means if you are a patient

If your embryologist mentions that some of your eggs had a thick shell, a grainy centre or a fragmented polar body, that is a description, not a diagnosis. The current international expert consensus considers eggs with most of these appearances suitable for use.<sup>[3](#ref3)</sup>

It also means that the appearance of your eggs is not the whole story, in either direction. Eggs that look perfect can still carry chromosomal errors, and eggs that look unusual can still become healthy embryos. Your age, and how your embryos go on to develop in the days after fertilisation, usually tell your clinic more than how the eggs looked on the day of collection.

Reasonable questions to ask your clinic: how many of my eggs were mature, how does the lab record egg appearance, and did anything they saw change how my eggs or embryos were handled? A good clinic will explain what it saw and how much weight it placed on it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you tell egg quality by looking at it under a microscope?

Only partly. An embryologist can see whether an egg is mature and can note features such as the shell, the polar body and the cytoplasm. But a systematic review of 50 studies found that none of these features was unanimously shown to predict an egg's developmental competence, and chromosomal normality is not visible in a standard light-microscope image of the egg.

### What does a mature (MII) egg look like?

A mature, metaphase II egg has released its first polar body — a small cell visible between the egg and its outer shell, the zona pellucida. Maturity is the most practically important thing assessed at egg collection, because only mature eggs are normally injected during ICSI.

### Does a dark or grainy egg mean poor quality?

Not necessarily. The 2025 ESHRE/ALPHA Istanbul consensus update found the evidence insufficient to support a negative prognosis for eggs with cytoplasmic granularity and considers them suitable for clinical use. It described judgements of cytoplasmic colour as highly subjective.

### Are eggs with smooth endoplasmic reticulum aggregates (SER-a) discarded?

Practice has changed. Earlier guidance advised against using them; the 2025 consensus update states they could be inseminated based on case-by-case evaluation, with embryos from unaffected eggs generally prioritised. A review of 112 reported births after transfers involving such embryos found 101 healthy babies.

### Does age affect egg quality more than appearance?

Yes, as far as current evidence shows. In a study of 15,169 embryo biopsies, the chance that a patient had no chromosomally normal embryo was 2–6% between ages 26 and 37, 33% at 42 and 53% at 44 — a difference that is not visible under a standard microscope.

### Can AI predict which eggs will become good embryos?

To a limited extent so far. One of the largest studies, using 37,133 oocyte images, predicted blastocyst formation with an AUC of 0.63 on external data — a real but modest signal. AI oocyte assessment has not been shown to improve pregnancy or live birth rates, and it should be treated as decision support rather than a prediction of outcome.
